Declared Obligations

Defects 5 and 6 are the last two open from the recurring-payments audit, and neither is a coding error — both follow from inferring obligations out of transactions. This scopes the change that dissolves them: giving an obligation a row of its own.

Closes Defects 5 and 6 New tables none — scheduled_payments already exists Migration 018 Date 15 October 2026
Why these two are different

One root cause, not two bugs

Defect 5 — an obligation is invisible until its first payment. You cannot schedule a bill you have never paid, and a cancelled subscription keeps projecting forward until someone unflags it.

Defect 6 — the description is the series identity. Renaming one transaction silently splits a series in two; naming two unrelated bills alike silently merges them.

Both exist because a series is a GROUP BY over transactions rather than a thing. Patching the grouping cannot fix either: a smarter key still can't represent a bill with no payments, and a series with no id can't survive a rename. They dissolve together the moment an obligation has a row and a primary key.

The good news

toObligation is the seam

Everything downstream of it — projection, settlement, status, period and member grouping, the accordion — operates on the obligation shape and has no idea where that shape came from.

Flagged transactions → inferred series ↘ toObligation() → buildSchedule → settle → status → UI
scheduled_payments row → linked transactions ↗ same seam → unchanged from here on
The Dashboard does not change. Adopting declared obligations means adding a second producer of the obligation shape, not rewriting the pipeline. That is the payoff from collapsing the three scheduling engines into one — the work is now concentrated at one function and the screens that create rules, rather than spread across every surface.

Plan

Five phases

Numbered because the order is load-bearing: each phase is unsafe to start before the one before it has landed and been verified against real data.

1

Schema

Small
One migration, reversible, no data written.
  • Widen the frequency CHECK to cover semi-monthly and quarterly.
  • Add anchor_date, ends_on, person; drop NOT NULL from next_date and stop treating it as truth.
  • Replace own-only RLS with household SELECT / own-only writes, following migration 017's hardened form.
  • Add transactions.obligation_id with ON DELETE SET NULL — deleting a rule must never delete financial history — plus an index.

2

Backfill

Riskiest
Turn every inferred series into a real row, then link its transactions. This is the only step that writes to real financial data.
  • Reuse the tested detectFrequency from the app rather than reimplementing median-gap detection in SQL — a second detector would repeat the mistake defect 3 just fixed.
  • Dry-run mode first: print every rule it would create and every transaction it would link, and change nothing.
  • Idempotent, so a partial run can be repeated safely.
  • Run scripts/db/backup-local.sh immediately before the real pass.
scripts/db/backfill-obligations.mjs
3

Derivation

Medium
A second producer of the obligation shape, so declared rules flow through the pipeline that already exists.
  • obligationFromRule(rule, linkedTransactions) alongside toObligation.
  • Settlement matches on obligation_id when present, falling back to nearest-due-date for anything still unlinked.
  • Expected amount comes from rule.amount; the median stays only as the fallback for inferred series.
  • is_active = false and ends_on stop projection without hiding history.

4

Rule management

Largest
The user-facing half, and the only phase that changes what people do.
  • The Recurring screen becomes an obligation manager: create, edit, pause, resume, delete rules. Today it creates transactions.
  • A bill can finally be scheduled before it is ever paid — defect 5, closed.
  • NewTransactionModal's is_recurring checkbox becomes an obligation picker: "counts toward <obligation>".
  • Dashboard, notifications and Expenses need no changes.

Needs your call

Five decisions I should not make alone

Each changes what gets built. My recommendation is on each, but these are judgement calls about your money and your data.

1 · How far back should the backfill reach?

Every series that has ever existed, or only those seen in the last N months? Backfilling everything resurrects cancelled subscriptions as active obligations projecting into the future — which is the cancelled half of defect 5, reintroduced by the fix for it.

Recommend  Backfill all of it for history, but set is_active = false on any series with no payment in the last two cycles. Nothing is lost, and nothing phantom projects.

2 · Does an obligation belong to a person or to the household?

Today a shared bill is one transaction stream owned by whoever pays it, tagged Shared. Family subtotals attribute it wholly to the payer. A household-owned obligation would model it properly — and is a materially bigger change.

Recommend  Keep user_id ownership for now and carry person across for the label. Treat shared-cost splitting as its own piece of work rather than smuggling it in here.

3 · What happens to is_recurring?

It becomes derivable from obligation_id IS NOT NULL. Dropping it means touching six call sites including the transaction tag and the import paths; keeping it means a denormalised flag that can drift from the link.

Recommend  Keep it through phase 4, drop it in phase 5. Dropping it mid-migration multiplies the risk of the one phase that writes real data.

4 · How should genuinely variable bills behave?

Hydro is never the same twice. A declared amount makes it read as Partially paid whenever it lands more than 2% low, and Paid-with-change when it lands high. The median heuristic handled this accidentally well.

Recommend  An is_variable flag on the rule: the declared amount becomes an estimate, and settlement treats any payment in the cycle as settling it in full.

5 · Do one-off obligations belong in this pass?

The frequency enum already allows once, but the projection assumes repetition throughout. Supporting it means a special case in occurrence generation.

Recommend  Defer. Reject once at the UI for now and keep the projection uniform. A one-off bill is adequately served by a plain transaction.

Risk

Where this can go wrong

Phase 2 is the one to be careful with. It writes to 600-plus real transactions in a live financial record. Everything else is additive or reversible. Mitigations: dry-run that prints and changes nothing, a backup immediately before, idempotent writes, and a down migration that drops the link column without touching a single transaction's money.

Backfill mis-detects a frequency and creates a wrong scheduleDry-run output reviewed before the real pass; frequency is editable afterwards, and correcting a rule does not touch any transaction.

Unchanged

What this does not touch

✓The Dashboard. Upcoming Payments, the accordion, totals and statuses all consume the obligation shape and never learn where it came from.
✓Financial totals. A transaction stays the only record that carries money. Obligations remain a projection and never enter Income or Expenses.
✓Currency handling. toBase() and buildRateMap() are untouched.
✓History. No transaction is edited, moved or deleted — the backfill only sets a link column.
✓Expenses, Reports, Budgets, Calendar. No changes at all.
If you only do part of it

Where to stop

Phases 1 to 3 are worth doing on their own: they add the records, the link and the derivation without changing a single screen. At that point a declared obligation works end-to-end — it just has no UI to create one, so the only rules that exist are the ones the backfill made.

Phase 4 is where the user-visible value lands, and it is the largest piece. Phase 5 is cleanup; leaving it undone costs a redundant code path, not correctness.

Stopping after phase 3 is a coherent place to be. Stopping mid-phase-2 is not — that leaves half the series linked and half inferred.
